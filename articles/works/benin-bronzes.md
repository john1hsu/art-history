# Benin Bronzes

"Benin Bronzes" is a loose label for thousands of brass and bronze works made for the royal court of the Kingdom of Benin, in what is now Edo State, Nigeria. The group includes relief plaques, commemorative heads, figures, bells and regalia, and it is often extended to cover related ivory and wood pieces. Most surviving metalwork dates from the fifteenth to the nineteenth century, although the kingship itself was formalized around the thirteenth century. Today the objects are as widely discussed for how they left Africa as for how they were made.

## Making the works

Benin's brass heads were made by the lost-wax process. The Digital Benin catalogue entry for a commemorative head in the Metropolitan Museum describes it: a carving in beeswax or a similar material is covered in layers of clay, firing melts the wax out and leaves a hollow ceramic mold, hot metal is poured in, and the mold is broken away once the metal cools. Each cast is therefore unique. The Metropolitan Museum's survey notes that Benin's metal-casting techniques were likely introduced by Yoruba neighbors, and it records a tradition that in the fourteenth century Benin's king asked the ruler of Ife to send a bronze smith. Whatever the origin, Benin developed its own large and distinctive body of royal art over roughly five hundred years.

Production was organized through guilds attached to the palace of the Oba, or king. Britannica describes the works as the product of these court guilds, and the Pitt Rivers Museum notes separate guilds of brass-casters, ivory-carvers, iron-workers, weavers and wood-carvers. The works were first of all instruments of royal ceremony.

## Imagery and function

Brass heads were reserved for the royal ancestral altars of past obas. According to the Digital Benin entry, each altar was dedicated to a specific king, and before becoming oba a successor commissioned a portrait of his predecessor for it. The Pitt Rivers Museum adds that altar heads honored the dead and helped contact their spirits, and that their material signaled the owner's status: brass for royal altars, wood for chiefs, and terracotta for brass-casters. The same entry describes the idealized youthful face typical of Benin leaders, crosshatching that stands for a beaded crown, and horizontal neck lines for a coral bead necklace. Late heads with an elongated collar and wing-like crown projections are associated with the nineteenth century.

The brass plaques once decorated the Oba's palace and were made by the brass-casters' guild, according to the Pitt Rivers Museum. That museum's account also explains several recurring motifs. The leopard symbolizes royal power; in the seventeenth century the Oba paraded tame leopards in chains to show his mastery of the "King of the Bush." The mudfish stands for peace, prosperity and fertility, and for the Oba's position between the human world and the spirit world of Olokun, the god of water. Coral is a royal material, and possession of the royal coral beads determines who sits on the throne.

## The 1897 expedition and dispersal

In January 1897 a British trade mission headed for Benin City was attacked, and seven British delegates and a large number of African carriers died, according to the British Museum. In February British forces captured the city. The palace was burned in part, the Oba was exiled, several chiefs were executed, and thousands of ceremonial and ritual objects were taken, including more than 900 brass plaques. Benin City then remained under British imperial control until 1960.

The loot spread quickly. Some objects were sold to help cover expedition costs, and others went to museums and private collectors. That autumn the British Museum displayed 304 plaques lent by the Foreign Office and later received 203 of them as a donation. Britannica reports that the British Museum holds the largest collection, while Nigerian museum authorities estimate that over five thousand objects are now spread across roughly 160 institutions, mainly in Europe and North America, though that figure is a Nigerian institutional estimate and is not given with detailed citations.

## Restitution debates

Calls for return have continued for decades, and positions differ. In October 2021 Nigeria's federal ministry of information and culture sent the British Museum a written request for the return of Nigerian antiquities, and representatives of the Benin royal palace have also asked for the collections back. The British Museum says it works with the palace and Nigeria's National Commission for Museums and Monuments through the Benin Dialogue Group, supports a planned permanent display in Benin City, and takes part in the Digital Benin database. It does not state a decision to return objects in its published account; it also records that in 1950 and 1951 it de-accessioned 26 plaques, 25 of which went to Nigeria. Nigerian sources, by contrast, argue that progress by other institutions highlights British delay: they report that in 2022 Berlin's Ethnologisches Museum transferred ownership of 512 objects to Nigeria and that the Smithsonian announced the return of 29 bronzes. The Oba himself said in 2018 that objects shown abroad could also serve as cultural ambassadors, so even within Benin the question of where works should be seen is not framed in a single way.
