# The Shahnama of Shah Tahmasp

## A king's book of kings

The *Shahnama*, or "Book of Kings," is the Persian national epic, composed by the poet Firdausi around the year 1000. It traces the history of Iranian kingship from a mythic age of first rulers, through the exploits of heroes, to a more historical stretch of dynasties, and it dwells as much on love, loss and death as on battle. Because the poem is secular and tied so closely to Iranian identity, it was copied again and again, often with pictures. Among all those copies, the manuscript made for the Safavid court in the early sixteenth century is generally regarded as the most ambitious ever produced.

## Commission and workshop

The book was made in the royal workshop at Tabriz, the first Safavid capital. Scholars still debate precisely when it was made, but work probably began in the early 1520s for Shah Isma'il I, the founder of the dynasty, and then continued for at least two decades under his son Shah Tahmasp, who became its principal patron and to whom it is dedicated. The Metropolitan Museum dates its production broadly to 1520–40.

Such a project demanded a large, stable workshop. Sultan Muhammad, Mir Musavvir and Aqa Mirak are named as successive directors of the enterprise, and many other painters, calligraphers, illuminators and gold-workers contributed. Individual pages were frequently the product of several hands, so attributions tend to point to a workshop as much as to a single master.

## Scale and style

The finished volume held roughly 759 folios of text in flowing *nasta'liq* script, each page about 48 by 32 centimetres, with gold-flecked margins and rich illumination. It contained 258 paintings, an extraordinary number for a single book. Stylistically it fused two traditions: the lively, brightly coloured manner of Turkmen painting in Tabriz and Shiraz, and the more restrained palette and balanced compositions of the Timurid school of Herat. Out of that meeting came a distinctly early Safavid idiom, with the Herat influence especially visible in some of the later pictures.

## The Court of Gayumars

A good illustration is *The Court of Gayumars*, made around 1524–25 and attributed to Sultan Muhammad's circle. It shows the legendary first king seated on a mountaintop with his son Siyamak and grandson Hushang, while courtiers in leopard skins and a menagerie of animals gather below. The composition climbs a vertical axis, mixes several viewpoints, and hides tiny faces in the rocks and foliage. Swirling clouds reflect the long reach of Chinese pictorial ideas into Persian painting. The king's gesture toward Siyamak anticipates the young man's coming death, a reminder that the scene is less a peaceful idyll than the opening of a tragedy. Painting and calligraphy interlock on the page, though text and image do not always match closely.

## Gift, dispersal and legacy

The manuscript did not remain in Iran. When Selim II came to the Ottoman throne, an Iranian embassy reached Edirne in February 1568 bearing lavish presents, and the *Shahnama* was among them. It stayed in the Topkapı Palace library in Istanbul until the early twentieth century; Ottoman Turkish inserts describing the paintings were added around 1800.

Later it passed into private hands, including those of Baron Edmund de Rothschild and then Arthur A. Houghton Jr., who broke the book apart and distributed its leaves. Houghton gave a group of pages to the Metropolitan Museum in 1970, and the Met now holds 78 painted pages. Other folios, including *The Court of Gayumars*, are in collections such as the Aga Khan Museum in Toronto, while some have appeared at auction. The dispersal means the book can no longer be seen as its makers intended, but each surviving page still shows the ambition of a court that treated a poem as a monument.
