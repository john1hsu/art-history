# Bada Shanren (Zhu Da)

Bada Shanren, born Zhu Da around 1626 in Nanchang, Jiangxi province, and dead by about 1705, is among the most original painters in Chinese history. A descendant of the Ming imperial family who lived through the Manchu conquest, he spent decades as a Buddhist monk before turning to painting for a living. His sparse, unsettling ink pictures of fish, birds and lotuses have come to stand for the Chinese tradition of the eccentric individualist.

## A prince after the fall of the dynasty

Zhu Da grew up in a family connected to the Ming ruling house and was reportedly precocious in poetry, calligraphy, seal carving and painting. He was educated for the official career that his lineage might have promised, but the Ming collapsed in 1644, when he was about eighteen, and his father died around the same time. Accounts describe a lasting speech difficulty and deep distress after these events. Like many people with ties to the old court, he was in a precarious position under the new Qing government, and withdrawing from public life was both a refuge and a statement.

## Monk, crisis, and a new name

In the late 1640s he entered a Buddhist monastery and became a Chan (Zen) monk, eventually teaching students of his own. Around 1680 he left the monastic life. Sources differ on exact dates, and the Metropolitan Museum places his first use of the name Bada Shanren in 1684, while the National Gallery of Victoria dates it to 1685. Britannica notes that he used several names, and the NGV essay counts more than forty over his lifetime.

Around this time he went through a period of apparent breakdown. Stories tell of him laughing and weeping in public, tearing his robes, running through a market in strange clothes, and writing the character for "mute" on his door and refusing to speak. Historians cannot say whether this was true illness, eccentric temperament, or a calculated performance. ## The yimin and the individualists

Zhu Da is classed with his contemporary Shitao as one of the Individualist painters of the early Qing. He is also associated with the *yimin*, "leftover subjects" who remained loyal to the fallen dynasty. His work is often read as coded protest: a bird glaring from a rock, or a lonely fish in empty space, can be understood as an image of a survivor with no place left in the world. Such readings are interpretive, and the paintings never state their meaning outright, which is part of their power.

## Style

Bada's mature manner is immediately recognizable. He used ink in a loose, wet, calligraphic way, reducing his subjects to a few confident strokes and leaving most of the paper bare. Birds and fish often glower, with a defiant, sometimes perverse air that Britannica calls characteristic. Compositions tilt or float without a ground line, so the blank paper itself reads as water, air or void.

His *Fish and Rocks* of 1699 in the Metropolitan Museum shows the method: seven small fish are suspended in empty paper beside two rocks, with no horizon to anchor the scene. The Met's text describes it as treating the picture as a calligraphic design built from contrasts of large and small, solid and empty. He also wrote calligraphy of striking, rounded simplicity, and the writing and the paintings share one sensibility.

Landscape came late. He painted few until nearly seventy, then made it a major part of his output in the last decade of his life. An album of landscapes datable to 1698 shows faceted rocks, calligraphic trees and generous empty space. He looked back to Dong Qichang and to earlier masters such as Dong Yuan, Guo Xi and Huang Gongwang, but departed from them boldly.

## Afterlife

Bada Shanren's reputation grew in the twentieth century, when painters searching for an alternative to academic convention found a model in him. Zhang Daqian collected his works and in 1940 made pictures in his manner, including *Blissful Old Age*, now in the Freer Gallery of Art, which imitates Bada's dry, rough brushwork. Qi Baishi was another admirer. A frog picture of 1950 in the Metropolitan Museum draws on Bada's loose brushwork and bold composition, though Qi softened the older painter's austerity with warmth and humor.
