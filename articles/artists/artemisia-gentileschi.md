# Artemisia Gentileschi

Artemisia Gentileschi (born in Rome in 1593, died in Naples after 1654) was one of the most accomplished painters of the Italian Baroque and among the first women admitted to Florence's Accademia delle Arti del Disegno. Her large, dramatic history paintings, often centered on determined or imperiled women, earned her patrons at the courts of Florence and England, and her reputation, which waned in the eighteenth century, has been rebuilt by modern scholarship and exhibitions.

## Training and early work

Because women were excluded from most formal artistic training, Artemisia learned in the workshop of her father, the painter Orazio Gentileschi. Orazio belonged to the generation of artists inspired by Caravaggio, and he taught his daughter to work directly from live models. Artemisia became a second-generation Caravaggist: she took over the stark lighting, the physical immediacy of the figures, and the willingness to stage biblical violence without idealizing it. Where Orazio's version of the style tends toward lyrical elegance, hers is more tense and concentrated. Her earliest known work, *Susanna and the Elders* (1610), was for a long time attributed to her father. She also studied with Agostino Tassi, a landscape and perspective specialist who worked with Orazio.

## The 1612 trial

In 1611 Tassi raped the teenage Artemisia. Orazio brought charges the following year, and the trial that resulted survives in transcripts, which record that she was subjected to torture during questioning to test her testimony. Tassi was found guilty and sentenced to exile from Rome, but the sentence was not enforced. Scholars now caution against reading her paintings only as autobiography; the episode matters for understanding her circumstances, but her skill as a painter and storyteller stands on its own.

## Florence and the Judiths

Soon after the trial Artemisia moved to Florence, where she refined her technique and built a professional network. In 1616 she became one of the first women accepted into the Accademia delle Arti del Disegno (the Royal Collection Trust calls her the first), a milestone that gave her standing and access to patrons. Among her best-known works from this period are the two versions of *Judith Slaying Holofernes*, one now in the Capodimonte museum in Naples and one in the Uffizi in Florence. The Uffizi version is associated with the Medici court under Grand Duke Cosimo II. Caravaggio's earlier treatment of the subject was an important model, but Artemisia shifted the emphasis from the victim's scream to the physical effort of the two women, Judith and her maid Abra, who work together with braced arms and a firm grip. Technical studies indicate the Naples picture is the earlier of the two.

## Later career

Artemisia later worked in Rome, Venice, and above all Naples, where she returned for much of her last decades. In 1638 she traveled to London, probably to assist her aging father with ceiling paintings at the Queen's House in Greenwich. Charles I had been trying to attract her to London for several years, and her father had worked for the royal couple since 1626. Orazio died soon after, and she remained active at the English court until about 1640. Inventories of Charles I's collection list seven paintings by her. For a long time only one was thought to survive in the Royal Collection: *Self-Portrait as the Allegory of Painting* (La Pittura), which shows her at work, brow damp and apron over her dress, as both a woman and the personification of the art. More recently, curators identified a *Susanna and the Elders* in the Royal Collection by matching it to an inventory record and the royal brand on the canvas.

## Reputation

Artemisia was admired in her lifetime, especially for her portraits, but her fame declined in the eighteenth century, and women artists were largely overlooked by art history until the twentieth. She has received renewed critical attention since the middle of the twentieth century, and recent major exhibitions have established her as a central figure of the Baroque. Today her history paintings are valued for their naturalism and for placing female experience and agency at the center of well-known stories, including Cleopatra, Lucretia, Bathsheba, and Judith.
