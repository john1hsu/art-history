# Encaustic Painting

Encaustic is a painting method in which pigment is bound in wax and worked while the wax is warm. The word comes from a Greek term connected with burning or heating, and heat is the defining feature: the medium is melted, brushed or troweled onto a support, and often fused or manipulated with heated implements so that it sets into a dense, luminous layer. Its most famous surviving products are the funerary portraits made in Egypt under Roman rule, and its best-known modern revival came in the 1950s.

## What the medium is

The binder is beeswax, sometimes thought to have been modified or mixed with other materials. Because wax cools quickly, the painter works in short, deliberate passes. Where the paint was applied hot and shaped with tools, the result is thick, textured and sculptural, with visible strokes and tool marks. These traces are the main physical evidence scholars use to say that a painting is truly encaustic rather than merely painted with wax. A Getty publication on the mummy portraits argues for caution on exactly this point: wax-based paintings that show no signs of heat application should not automatically be called encaustic, and may simply have been painted with molten wax.

## Ancient references

Greek writers and Pliny the Elder are the main literary witnesses. An EBSCO research overview notes that the Greeks were skilled in panel painting in encaustic as well as tempera, citing Pliny and the later writer Pausanias. Pliny's own remarks are hard to use as a recipe. The Roman author mentions a material called "Punic wax," but his description is cryptic: modern readers have interpreted it as purified beeswax, or as wax treated with an alkali so it could be thinned with water. Scholars have proposed several recipes mixing wax with resin, egg or oil, yet the Getty account notes that none has been confirmed by scientific analysis of the portraits themselves. The written sources show wax painting was known and prized, but not exactly how painters prepared it.

## The Fayum portraits

The strongest body of evidence is a group of roughly a thousand portraits on thin wooden panels from Roman-period Egypt, which Harvard Art Museums dates to the first to fourth century CE; the Brooklyn Museum gives a range of 95 to 230 CE for its examples. They are called Fayum portraits after the Fayum Oasis, south of Cairo, where most were found. The panels were placed over the face of the deceased and woven into the linen wrapping, functioning like a mask. Brooklyn describes the style as Greco-Roman, with realistic, individualized faces, shading and Roman clothing, while the placement over the face comes from Egyptian funerary practice. Harvard Art Museums notes that hairstyle and dress can sometimes help date a panel, and that its examples are on wood such as linden, which is not native to Egypt and was probably imported.

Scholars have traditionally divided the portraits into an encaustic group and a tempera group, originally by appearance. Encaustic examples tend to show thick, textured paint and tool marks, while tempera examples are flatter and matte. The Getty study of two portraits in the Art Institute of Chicago found beeswax in both, though one had the heavy impasto expected of heat-applied wax and the other showed the fine, crosshatched handling of tempera. Harvard reports that its tempera portrait used animal glue as the binder, and the Getty account adds that most non-wax examples seem to contain glue rather than egg. Only a small number of portraits have been studied rigorously, so many attributions still rest on how the surface looks.

## Decline

The mummy-portrait custom is dated to the first four centuries CE, running, in the Getty catalogue's words, from Julio-Claudian times through the age of Constantine. None of the sources consulted explains why it ended. Wax painting itself continued: a sixth-century encaustic icon of the Virgin and Child, belonging to the Monastery of Saint Catherine at Sinai and lent to the Metropolitan Museum, is catalogued as Early Byzantine work from Egypt, and Smarthistory describes the sixth-century *Christ Blessing* icon there as encaustic, a technique used in Egypt from at least the late first century CE. Both date from well before the image controversy described in [Byzantine Iconoclasm](../themes/byzantine-iconoclasm.md). Later Sinai icons, such as a late twelfth-century *Heavenly Ladder* and works of the early thirteenth century, are in tempera and gold, and the tempera tradition in turn peaked from about the thirteenth century to the mid-1500s before declining with oil paint, according to EBSCO. These sources show encaustic giving way to other media over many centuries, but they do not assign a single cause or date to its decline.

## Modern revival

Encaustic returned to prominence in twentieth-century American art through Jasper Johns. His *Flag* (1954–55), in the Museum of Modern Art, is described by the museum as encaustic, oil and collage on fabric mounted on plywood. Johns dipped strips of cloth and newsprint into hot wax and attached them to a bedsheet, and the process remains visible on the surface. He went on to use encaustic repeatedly for familiar subjects such as flags, targets, numbers, letters and maps. The fast-setting, textured wax suited his interest in treating a recognizable image as a physical object, which links an ancient funerary craft to postwar painting.
