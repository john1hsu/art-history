# Inca Qompi Tapestry Weaving and Tocapu

In the Inca Empire, the most valuable thing a person could make was not a metal object but a piece of cloth. The finest Inca textile was *qompi* (also spelled *cumbi*), a tapestry-woven fabric produced for the state and for the sacred sphere. It stood apart from everyday cloth, and its surface carried a system of small geometric designs known as *tocapu* (*t'oqapu*) that continues to provoke debate.

## Fibre and fineness

Andean weavers worked with two main fibres. Cotton grew in the warm coastal valleys, while llamas, alpacas, vicuñas and guanacos lived in the highlands. Camelid hair accepts dye far more readily than cotton, so it was the natural choice for the saturated colours that high-status cloth demanded. Cochineal reds and indigo blues were especially prized: cochineal needs vast numbers of insects for a small quantity of dye, and good indigo requires time and expertise.

Qompi pushed the process to an extreme. Museum documentation of the best-known surviving example, a royal tunic at Dumbarton Oaks, describes undyed cotton warps carrying dyed camelid wefts spun so finely and packed so tightly that roughly a hundred wefts occupy each centimetre. The result is a smooth, dense, almost printed-looking surface that is nonetheless light and strong.

## Weavers and state control

Before a single weft was inserted, enormous labour went into collecting fibre, spinning and dyeing, which is part of why finished qompi was so costly. The finest cloth was woven by specialists, including the *aqllakuna* or "chosen women" gathered from across the empire and housed in residential weaving centres, and by skilled male weavers. Such workshops existed in the capital and at outposts elsewhere. The state regulated who could make qompi, what thread went into it, how it was decorated and who could wear it; only a narrow elite wore the best cloth.

Garments were typically woven as a single rectangle with a central slit for the head, then folded and sewn at the sides. Weaving a tunic in one piece was a deliberate choice: Andean tradition held that cutting cloth from the loom damaged its animating spirit.

## Cloth as wealth, gift and offering

Textiles were valued above gold or gems. Rulers gave qompi garments to nobles, successful military leaders and the lords of conquered territories, a gift that formed part of an unequal but reciprocal relationship binding local elites to the Inca. Cloth was also an offering. Fine textiles decorated sacred places, and royal garments were burned as sacrifices, including in ceremonies connected to the sun deity Inti and to funerary rites for rulers.

## Tocapu

Tocapu are small rectangular units filled with lines, triangles, crosses, stepped forms and spirals, often arranged symmetrically. They differ from the animals, plants and human figures that dominated earlier Andean textile imagery. On most tunics they appear as a band at the waist or neck, or in a few rows; the more tocapu a garment carried, the higher the prestige it conveyed. A checkerboard pattern, linked to the army, signalled military standing.

What the motifs meant remains unsettled. For several decades many scholars have suggested that individual tocapu may have referred to peoples, places or social roles, and some have proposed a lost writing system. Others, notably the scholar Andrew Hamilton, argue that this reading lacks evidence and that tocapu worked more like a controlled emblem system, an imperial "logo wear" that advertised the state and its ruler. The question stays open partly because no key survives and almost no securely dated examples exist.

## The Dumbarton Oaks tunic

The all-tocapu tunic at Dumbarton Oaks in Washington, D.C., is covered with more of these motifs than any other known Inca garment, which has led to its interpretation as a royal piece, possibly made for the last independent emperor, Atahualpa. It is unfinished: needle holes show that a multicoloured zigzag hem was begun but never completed. Later repairs are cruder than the original weaving, suggesting that the skilled hands who made it were no longer available.

## After the Conquest

Weaving did not end in 1532. Indigenous weavers continued to produce tapestry textiles under Spanish rule, blending Inca motifs with European ones, such as lattice patterns and imitation lace on women's mantles, and adopting new materials like sheep's wool, metallic thread and European looms. Tocapu became more widely available and spread across grounds that earlier garments had left plain, though colonial versions were typically woven on a larger scale with thicker thread and simpler designs. Today weavers around Cusco still reproduce tocapu grids on Spanish-style floor looms for the tourist market, continuing a tradition that began as an instrument of imperial power.
