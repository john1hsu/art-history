# Ukiyo-e: Pictures of the Floating World

Ukiyo-e, usually translated as "pictures of the floating world," is the name given to the paintings and above all the woodblock prints that were made for the townspeople of Edo-period Japan. The "floating world" referred to the fleeting pleasures of city life: theaters, teahouses, pleasure quarters, festivals, and fashionable people. Between roughly the late seventeenth and the nineteenth century, the tradition turned these subjects into one of the most widely circulated visual cultures of its time, and later into one of the most influential exports of Japanese art.

## Origins

The style grew out of book illustration. As commercial publishing expanded in Edo (modern Tokyo), especially after a devastating fire in 1657 led to rebuilding and a boom in printed matter, pictures began to detach from the texts they accompanied and to be sold as independent images. Hishikawa Moronobu is generally credited as the founder of the tradition; in the later seventeenth century he produced illustrated books and then single sheets, and by around 1700 the single-sheet print had become the dominant form. Early prints were printed in black ink, sometimes colored by hand, and were followed by limited palettes of red and green before a major technical leap in the 1760s.

## How a print was made

A print was never the work of one person. A publisher commissioned the design, paid for it, decided how many copies to produce, and handled sales; in the later period the design was also checked by government censors. A designer drew the image in ink on thin paper. A carver pasted that drawing face down on a smooth block of cherrywood and cut away the wood around the brushed lines, which destroyed the original drawing in the process. A printer then inked the raised lines and pressed paper against the block by hand to make a black-line proof.

Color required more blocks. The designer marked a proof with the intended colors, and the carver cut a separate block for each one. Small notches cut into every block gave the printer fixed points against which to align the paper, so each color landed in exactly the right place; colors were generally printed one at a time, typically beginning with the lightest. An ordinary multicolor print therefore needed about ten impressions, a process called nishiki-e or "brocade pictures," which emerged in the 1760s and is associated with Suzuki Harunobu. Because stored blocks could be used again, a popular design could be reprinted many times, although the lines gradually lost their sharpness as the wood wore down.

## Subjects and artists

Early and classic ukiyo-e centered on beautiful women (bijin-ga) and on kabuki actors, whose portraits functioned partly as fan merchandise. Kitagawa Utamaro refined the half-length portrait of women, while Tōshūsai Sharaku produced intense, sometimes unflattering actor likenesses in a very short career in the 1790s. Erotic prints (shunga) were also a significant part of the market. Warriors, literary and historical scenes, and witty parodies of classical themes filled out the repertoire.

In the first half of the nineteenth century, landscape became a major genre. Katsushika Hokusai's views of Mount Fuji and Utagawa Hiroshige's series on the Tōkaidō road and on the sights of Edo used travel, weather, and unexpected vantage points to make ordinary places feel dramatic. Utagawa Kuniyoshi was another leading figure of the later period, and Kobayashi Kiyochika, working in the Meiji era, is often regarded as one of the last major figures in the line.

## Decline and Western reception

After the Meiji Restoration of 1868, modernization and new photographic and printing technologies reduced the audience for traditional prints, and many were sold abroad. Japan's opening to Western trade in the 1850s had already begun to send woodblock prints to Europe, along with other Japanese goods, and Paris saw Japanese arts in quantity at the 1867 World's Fair. The resulting enthusiasm is known as japonisme.

Artists responded to specific qualities of the prints: asymmetrical compositions, flattened space, bold outlines, strong color areas, and cropped or high viewpoints. Edgar Degas adopted elongated formats and off-center arrangements. Mary Cassatt, after a large exhibition of Japanese prints in Paris in 1890, made a series of color etchings that echo Utamaro's attention to women's daily lives. Toulouse-Lautrec drew on kabuki prints for the color and contour of his posters, and Gauguin and the Nabis, including Bonnard and Vuillard, absorbed Japanese ideas of viewpoint and surface pattern. Ukiyo-e thus helped loosen European habits of illusionistic perspective and prepared the ground for much of modern art, even as the prints themselves were being reassessed in Japan.
