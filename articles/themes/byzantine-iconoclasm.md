# Byzantine Iconoclasm

Byzantine Iconoclasm, literally the "breaking of images," was a long dispute over whether sacred figures could properly be depicted and honored in paint, mosaic and other media. It unfolded in the eastern Roman Empire over roughly a century and a quarter and left a visible mark on the churches of Constantinople and beyond. It also helped define what an icon was understood to be.

## Images before the conflict

By the seventh century, images of Christ, the Virgin and the saints were woven into daily devotion. Panel paintings were carried in processions, kissed by worshippers and hung in homes, while mosaics glittered in apses and on church walls. Many believers treated an icon as a channel to the holy figure it showed, and the gold grounds typical of such works signified heaven rather than any earthly place. Church law had already touched the subject: a council held at Constantinople in 691–92 instructed artists to show Christ in his human form rather than as a symbolic lamb, a stress on his incarnation that later image-defenders would exploit.

## Two phases of prohibition

Tradition holds that the emperor Leo III (r. 717–41) launched the campaign around 726 or 730, supposedly by removing a Christ image from the Chalke Gate of the imperial palace. Modern historians doubt that story, and recent scholarship, notably the survey by Leslie Brubaker and John Haldon, questions many inherited assumptions by weighing written, archaeological and visual evidence together and by setting the dispute in its political and social context rather than treating it as purely theological. What is firmly documented is the zeal of his son Constantine V (r. 741–75), who summoned a council at Hieria in 754 that rejected sacred images.

The first phase ended after Empress Irene convened the Second Council of Nicaea in 787, which reversed Hieria and affirmed that images could be venerated. A second ban followed in 815 under Leo V, partly in the wake of military defeats by the Bulgars. This second phase was generally milder than the first and lasted until 843.

## The arguments

Opponents of images appealed to the biblical prohibition on graven images in the Ten Commandments. They also reasoned that God is invisible and infinite, so no picture could capture Christ, who was both human and divine. Practices such as lighting candles, burning incense, bowing before and kissing icons looked to them like worship offered to mere matter.

Defenders answered on several fronts. Scripture itself, they noted, records God commanding the making of cherubim for the Ark of the Covenant. More decisively, because God had become visible in the incarnation, depicting Christ's human form affirmed rather than denied that event. Thinkers such as John of Damascus distinguished between the honor owed to the person represented and the object that carried the likeness: veneration passed through the image to its prototype. Nicaea II adopted this logic.

## What happened to the art

The material record shows the controversy at work. In the Hagia Sophia, figural mosaics of Christ and the saints were removed from a chamber known as the sekreton during the later eighth century, and plain cross roundels now mark the spots, with erased inscriptions beneath them hinting at the names once displayed. At the Church of the Dormition in Nicaea, an apse mosaic of the Virgin and Child gave way to a simple cross, and a new image was installed after the restoration. In Constantinople's Hagia Eirene, rebuilt by Constantine V in the 750s after an earthquake, the apse still displays a large cross in gold and silver tesserae, its arms angled so that they read as straight from the floor below. Such aniconic decoration shows how artists kept sacred meaning alive without human figures.

## The Triumph of Orthodoxy

In 843 the empress Theodora and the official Theoktistos, ruling as regents for the young Michael III, restored images and ended the era. The Church remembered the moment as the Triumph of Orthodoxy, still observed on the first Sunday of Lent in the Eastern Orthodox calendar. The victory soon found visual expression: a Virgin and Child mosaic in the Hagia Sophia apse, dedicated in 867, was presented in its inscription and in Patriarch Photios's homily as a response to the iconoclast past.

The memory proved durable. A painted icon made around 1400 shows Theodora alongside an image of the Virgin Hodegetria held aloft by angels, with monks and clergy who suffered for defending images beneath. Made when the shrinking empire was desperate for help, it recalls an age when images seemed to protect the state, and it shows how thoroughly the Byzantines had come to define themselves through their sacred pictures.
